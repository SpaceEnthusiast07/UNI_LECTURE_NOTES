
#### Relational Algebra
As we all know that **algebra** is the study of mathematical symbols and the operations used to manipulate them.

Key terms:
 - **Operands** - data used in operations
 - **Operations** - certain rules used to manipulate operands

Therefore, **relational algebra** is where the operands are relations (tables), instead of integers in regular algebra, and operations specific to manipulating them.

*SQL*, or other DB querying languages, allow you to *define WHAT you want*, but *relational algebra* works behind the scenes and *defines HOW to retrieve the data* you want.


#### Unary Operations
**Projection ($\pi$)**
A **projection** of a relation $R$, denoted $\pi_{\text{<attribute list>}}(R)$, is a *new relation* (table) with a select *subset of attributes* (or columns) from the original relation $R$.
Since you have selected a subset of attributes from $R$, the relational schema (definition of relation) of the new relation will be different to that of $R$.

**Selection ($\sigma$)**
A **selection** of a relation $R$, denoted $\sigma_{\text{<selection conditions>}}(R)$, produces a *new relation* containing *tuples* from $R$ that *satisfy the selection condition*.
Since we are selecting tuples, the relational schema of the new relation is the same as that of $R$.

**Rename ($\rho$)**
To **rename** a relation $R$ to $S$, we use the formula: $\rho_{S}(R)$.
If we want to rename the relation $R$'s attributes, we specify them in brackets in the subtext, for example: $\rho_{(a1,a2,a3,...,an)}(R)$.
You can also rename the relation and its attributes at the same time.


#### Set Operations
All set operations manipulate tuples (rows) in relations; hence, the two relations being operated upon must have the same relational schema.

**Union ($R \cup S$)**
Produces a new relation that adds all tuples from $S$ onto the bottom of $R$; deleting duplicates if they occur. Hence, $R$ and $S$ must have the same relational schemas.

**Intersection ($R \cap S$)**
Produces a new relation containing tuples that are common (the same) between $R$ and $S$.

**Difference ($R - S$)**
Produces a new relation that has all the same tuples as $R$, but with all the tuples that are common with $S$ removed. The difference operator is not commutative; i.e.,  $R - S \neq S - R$.

**Cartesian Product ($R \times S$)**
Produces a new relation for which every tuple of $S$ is concatenated onto the right of every tuple of $R$. If $R$ and $S$ both contain attribute $A$, then $R.A$ and $S.A$ is used for the new attribute name. Below is a diagram to help you understand:

#### Binary Operations
All join operations are equivalent to the selection operator being applied to the cartesian product of the two relations.

**Theta Join ($\theta$-join)**
Defines a new relation that contains *all the combinations of tuples* from $R$ and $S$ (the cartesian product) that *satisfy a join condition*.
Notation: $R \bowtie_{<join condition>} S \equiv \sigma_{<join condition>}(R \times S)$.

**Equijoin**
This is the same as theta join, but you can only use equality comparisons within the join condition; i.e., equijoin is a special case of theta join.

**Natural Join**
The natural join uses the equijoin on all common attributes between $R$ and $S$. The resulting relation only contains one occurrence of each common attribute.

![[Pasted image 20261022154641.png|401]]

Q: List all copy information of each book.
A: $Book \bowtie BookCopy$

Q: List all available copy information of each book.
A: $(\sigma_{available='yes'}(BookCopy)) \bowtie Book$
