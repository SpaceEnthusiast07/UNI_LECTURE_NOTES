
#### The Entity-Relationship Model
One of the most challenging aspects of designing a database is that everyone, designers, programmers, and end-users, needs to see things in different ways.

Therefore, we need a model to *communicate the design of the database* in a *non-technical way* that is *free of ambiguities* (not open to interpretation)!

If you haven't already guessed it by now, the solution is the *Entity-Relationship Model*, the ER Model!!

There are different notations used to represent an Entity-Relationship Diagram, these are *Chen*, *Crow's Foot*, and *Unified Modelling Language (UML)* notation.

In this module, we are mostly interested in the *UML* notation, similar to the UML used in Year 1 OOP module.

Within an Entity-Relationship Diagram, you have different aspects: *Entities*, *Relationships*, *Attributes*, and *Constraints* (next lecture).


#### Entities
An **Entity** is a collection of objects with the same properties.

An **Entity** either has a *physical existence*, such as a student in a school, patient in a hospital, or department in a company; or a *conceptual existence*, such as a property viewing, school inspection, or car sale.

Entities aren't simply entities, you have two different types: *strong and weak*. You might be able to guess what each means, but a **strong entity** is an entity that *doesn't reply upon another entity to exist*, such as a patient in a hospital; patients exists no matter if the hospital exists or not. Then, a **weak entity** is the opposite, *an entity which only exists depending on another entity*, such as a preference; a preference cannot exist without a client/customer stating a preference.

#### Attributes
An **attribute** (or field) is *a property of an entity*, such as the name of a student or the birth date of a patient. An important note is that relationships can also have attributes. This is how attributes are represented in a UML ER diagram:
![[Pasted image 20261001123400.png|148]]

I must make this important note, attributes can be placed on relationships in the case where you require an attribute but can't decide which entity to store it in. You use a dashed line to the list of relationship attributes in a UML ER diagram.


#### Keys
Within ER diagrams, you have keys, which are specific named attributes that serve another purpose alongside their main job of being an attribute.

**Candidate keys** are *attributes that uniquely identify an entity instance*; an entity instance being a specific entity, such as a specific student in a table of students. Another requirement of the candidate key is that it must not contain null (missing) values. Furthermore, each entity must have at least one candidate key, but can have multiple.

The **primary key** is *one of the candidate keys that is selected to uniquely identify each occurrence of an entity* (entity instance). The most common primary key is the ID key of an entity, such as the studentID for a student. *Alternate keys* are all of the other candidate keys that weren't chosen as the primary key.

The foreign key is the primary key of another entity and forms part of the relationship between the two entities.
